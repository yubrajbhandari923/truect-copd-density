Literature review

Plan: plans/plan-001.yaml (section literature). Review date: 2026-11-12. Scope: the 12 seed papers listed in the plan, plus 13 primary papers added on CT-number-to-density conversion, deep-learning density and material estimation, how XCAT/DukeSim phantoms encode texture, and conditional diffusion compared with deterministic regression.

Reading rule. Every statement below comes from text that was actually read. For each paper the access level is given as full text or abstract only, and nothing is claimed beyond what that level supports. The “Not accessed” section lists the gaps.

1. Dataset and simulator facts (TrueCT and DukeSim)

This section collects the concrete facts needed to build training targets. The sources are Abadi et al. (2025) (full text, PMC11973969) and Abadi, Harrawood, et al. (2019) (full text, PMC6598436). Facts from the companion phantom papers are labelled with their own sources.

1.1 Phantom construction (emphysema cohort)

  • Cohort. The challenge used 200 models: 67 emphysema, 67 lung-lesion and 66 liver-lesion. The 67 emphysema models came from 67 randomly chosen COPDGene Phase I patient CTs (32 female; age 62 ± 9 years; BMI 28.7 ± 6.0 kg/m²) (Abadi et al. 2025). These are not drawn from the original XCAT library. Only the liver-lesion models were built from existing XCAT phantoms. The emphysema models were built from new segmentations in the XCAT style.
  • Segmentation. Major organs (body, bones, lungs, heart, liver) were segmented with commercial software. Airways, pulmonary vessels and emphysematous regions were segmented with a University of Iowa algorithm. The masks were meshed with VTK marching cubes, then smoothed and remeshed in Rhinoceros (“Smooth”, then “QuadRemesh”) (Abadi et al. 2025). Phantom boundaries are therefore smoothed meshes, not the original segmentation voxels.
  • Voxel size. All 200 models were voxelized at 0.25 mm isotropic (Abadi et al. 2025). Abadi, Segars, et al. (2019) gives the reason for this choice: 0.25 mm is about half the detector-element size of a clinical scanner.
  • Texture. “Trabecular bone texture and lung parenchyma were incorporated using anatomically informed statistical models” (Abadi et al. 2025). The challenge cites Abadi, Segars, et al. (2019), Abadi et al. (2018) and Abadi et al. (2017) for these models:
    • Trabecular bone (Abadi, Segars, et al. 2019, full text). Cortical bone is a morphological erosion of the bone mask, with thickness measured per phantom from its source CT. Trabecular texture comes from 3-D power-law noise synthesis, with spectral exponents fitted to Visible Human Female data and binned uniformly into five levels. In that paper’s simulation the five levels were assigned trabecular-bone densities of 1.00, 1.07, 1.14, 1.21 and 1.28 g/cm³. Trabecular density is therefore a small set of discrete density bins carried by separate voxel materials, not a continuous field.
    • Lung (Abadi et al. 2018, abstract; Abadi et al. 2017, abstract). Airways, arteries and veins were grown to terminal branches by a volume-filling algorithm. The 2018 paper reports about 46,000 airways, 45,000 arteries and 40,000 veins per phantom. The parenchyma is modelled as polyhedral secondary pulmonary lobules with interlobular-septa borders. Inside each lobule is a heterogeneous background from non-parametric texture synthesis, informed by a high-resolution lung-specimen CT. The number of parenchymal material bins was not stated in the abstracts read. It must be found from the phantom label tables in E00.
  • Material definition. Each organ or structure was assigned a density and an elemental composition from ICRU Report 46. Between patients, the “average density values (per ICRU report)” were varied “in the range of ± 15 HU” (Abadi et al. 2025). The emphysema regions were defined at −950 ± 17 HU (range −918 to −979 HU) (Abadi et al. 2025). The 67 lung-lesion models were built from a separate set of 67 COPDGene cases (Abadi et al. 2025). Pairing must confirm that the local 67 are the emphysema subset.
  • How emphysema density was set (precursor study). In the 20-phantom emphysema study by Abadi et al. (2023) (full text), emphysema voxels used the elemental composition of lung parenchyma. Their density was derived from the mean HU measured in the eroded emphysema mask of the source COPDGene CT, using ρ = μ / (μ/ρ)~lung parenchyma~. “Each XCAT phantom had one density assigned to the whole emphysema regions. The same approach was also used to assign lung density for the lung parenchyma for each case.” The ground-truth lung and emphysema values in that study were −880.0 ± 37.9 HU and −969.2 ± 22.7 HU. Inference: the TrueCT challenge reports emphysema HU per case. If it used the same method, emphysema density is one scalar per subject, taken from that subject’s material table. This must be confirmed against the local tables.
  • How materials reach the simulator. DukeSim takes two inputs: a voxelized phantom and a parameter file describing the scanner and protocol. Linear attenuation coefficients μm,E are computed per material and energy bin with xraylib, and ray-traced as path length times μ per material (Abadi, Harrawood, et al. 2019). The simulator interface is therefore material label plus material definition (composition and density), not a free-form density map. This is consistent with the plan’s DukeSIM contract. The exact local file format still has to be verified.

1.2 Acquisition (Duke-1 generic scanner)

From Abadi et al. (2025), Table 1 and §2.2:

Item Value
Detector Energy-integrating, Gd₂O₂S scintillator, 0.8 mm thick, 7.8 g/cm³, gain 50 photons/keV
Detector columns × rows 900 × 64
Projections per rotation 1000
Detector element / focal spot 1 × 1 mm / 1 × 1 mm
Source–isocenter / source–detector 575 mm / 1075 mm
Trajectory Helical, pitch 1.0
Spectrum 120 kV, measured bowtie profile
Tube current Tube current modulation (validated technique)
Dose levels 50–150 mAs in 25 mAs steps (5 levels; the per-case assignment is not tabulated in the paper)
Physics Ray-traced primary, Monte Carlo scatter, focal-spot wobble, anti-scatter grid, beam-hardening correction
Output DICOM-CT-PD sinograms, with per-projection tube current and water attenuation coefficient in the headers

DukeSim’s beam-hardening correction is a fourth-order polynomial water correction fitted from mono-energetic water simulations (Abadi, Harrawood, et al. 2019).

1.3 Reconstructions

  • The challenge shared sinograms, not images. Participants reconstructed the images themselves. The organisers reconstructed all 200 sinograms with the MCR toolkit, but only for quality control (artifact inspection). They also checked CT numbers on water-phantom and ACR-phantom simulations (Abadi et al. 2025).
  • Requested reconstruction format (Abadi et al. 2025, Table 2):
    • field of view 500 mm;
    • in-plane pixel size 500/512 ≈ 0.977 mm;
    • slice thickness 0.55 mm for chest cases (1.5 mm for liver cases);
    • slice interval equal to slice thickness, with no overlap;
    • full z-coverage;
    • DICOM format.
  • No kernel is specified. The five submitted methods differed widely: MBIR, Katsevich, FBP followed by algebraic (BiCGSTAB) updates, FDK, and fan-beam FBP with per-task ramp cut-offs at 90/60/40 % of Nyquist (Abadi et al. 2025). The paper does not say which reconstruction, if any, is redistributed with the dataset. The provenance, kernel and HU calibration of the local recons/ must be established in E00. Nothing in the literature fixes them.
  • HU calibration. The paper reports only that CT numbers were “verified in the water phantom and in the inserts of the ACR phantom” (Abadi et al. 2025). It gives no numerical calibration result.

1.4 Ground truth and scoring in the challenge

  • The digital ground truth was “the mono-energetic representation of the human models in HU at the effective energy (i.e., 64 keV)” of the acquisitions (Abadi et al. 2025). Abadi et al. (2023) used 67 keV for a Siemens Flash model, computed during the beam-hardening-correction step.
  • For scoring, reconstructions were upsampled to the 0.25 mm phantom grid by linear interpolation. RMSE and SSIM were computed in the lung-parenchyma VOI for emphysema cases. SSIM dynamic range was set to the ground-truth HU range (Abadi et al. 2025).
  • Emphysema results across the 5 participants: SSIM 0.22–0.78 and RMSE 77.6–186.7 HU (absolute range). Error depended on emphysema severity, because more severe cases had lower RMSE (Abadi et al. 2025).
  • The ground truth was withheld from participants. It “can be obtained by contacting the corresponding author” (Abadi et al. 2025).

1.5 Implications for target construction (inferences, to verify in E00)

  1. Density is largely piecewise-constant per material label. The exceptions are textured materials (trabecular bins, lung-parenchyma texture), which are encoded as additional labels, not as continuous values. A label-to-table density map (A07, label_to_table_density) is therefore physically natural. Density-only and label-only targets may carry nearly the same information, apart from the per-subject table values.
  2. The per-subject variation of ±15 HU around ICRU 46 values corresponds to small density changes for soft tissue. Its exact size depends on the material and has not been computed here. The per-subject emphysema density is a single scalar in a narrow range (−918 to −979 HU). Recovering subject-specific table entries from CT is a per-region mean-estimation problem, which voxelwise losses may not capture.
  3. The CT grid is about 0.98 × 0.98 × 0.55 mm and the phantom grid is 0.25 mm isotropic. At native phantom resolution, many of the roughly 130,000 vessel and airway branches per phantom (Abadi et al. 2018) are sub-voxel in CT. This supports keeping volume-fraction targets on the CT grid separate from native-grid labels (A14, A15).
  4. Reconstructed lung HU is biased relative to ground truth. Abadi et al. (2023) found the mean lung HU overestimated under every imaging condition, “mainly because of the presence of scatter”. Lung MAE was 57–175 HU, and errors were larger at edges and in denser regions. A purely physical HU-to-density baseline will therefore carry a systematic lung bias unless it is calibrated on TRAIN.

2. Per-paper notes: seed papers

Valand et al. (2026) (abstract and publisher page only)

  • Task: map spectral photon-counting CT images to a density map ρ and an effective atomic number map Zeff.
  • Input: simulated abdominal PCCT at 4 energy thresholds, 2 dose levels, several iodine concentrations and 3 clinical kernels.
  • Ground truth: the phantom truth of 32 training human models.
  • Model and loss: a GAN, trained with and without a “physics-informed regularization loss”. The loss is not defined in the abstract.
  • N: 32 training models; 16 test phantoms; 6 clinical cases; a reader study on 30 slices.
  • Headline results (with physics regularisation): NRMSE 1.29 % for ρ and 0.68 % for Zeff; SSIM 0.99 and 0.99; PSNR 29.8 and 29.04 dB. Maximum RMSE on clinical data was 5.45 %. In the reader study, median conspicuity was 4.52 for the material maps against 4.13 for virtual monochromatic images.
  • Clinical claim: the 6-case clinical check and the reader study. The authors note that the small sample limits generalisation.
  • Differences from our setting: spectral rather than single-energy input; abdomen rather than chest COPD; outputs are ρ and Zeff, not DukeSim labels. The physics loss relies on multi-energy measurements, so it should not be transferred.
  • Not read: architecture details, loss definitions, and any ablation of L1, Huber or gradient losses.

Abadi et al. (2025) (full text)

See §1. Task: benchmark CT reconstruction against phantom truth. There was no learning component, and no machine-learning submission was received. Clinical claim: none beyond being “modeled based on clinical CT images”. Difference from our setting: the challenge scored reconstruction against 64 keV HU truth; we invert reconstruction to materials and density.

Abadi, Harrawood, et al. (2019) (full text)

  • Task: a CT simulator for voxel phantoms.
  • Physics: ray-traced primary signal using per-material μ from xraylib. Scatter comes from a modified MC-GPU and is smoothed with a scatter kernel. The model also includes Gaussian quantum and electronic noise, detector crosstalk, 4 × 5 focal-spot/detector subsampling, flying focal spot, bowtie filter and polynomial water correction.
  • Validation: against a Mercury phantom on a Siemens Definition Flash at 120 kV, 50/150/300 mAs, reconstructed with FreeCT (medium kernel, 0.6 mm slices, 512² pixels, 370 mm FOV). Relative errors: contrast 1.4 %; noise magnitude below 0.5 %; NPS average and peak frequency 2.49 % and 2.57 %; MTF f50 3.1 % (air insert) and 0.4 % (bone insert).
  • Runtime: about 9 minutes per 3-rotation scan of a 1900 × 1900 × 1000-voxel, 0.25 mm phantom on 4 Titan Xp GPUs.
  • Difference from our setting: this is a forward model. It was validated on a cylindrical phantom only, and the authors list that as a limitation.

Salinas et al. (2025) (abstract only)

  • Task: the opposite direction to ours. Two 3-D Double-U-Net conditional GANs generate 16 organ textures from homogeneous segmentations, producing heterogeneous phantoms that are then simulated in DukeSim.
  • N: 378 CT–segmentation pairs from a public dataset, with 18 held out.
  • Results: MAD 46.15 ± 1.06 HU, SSIM 0.86, PSNR 28.62, MMD 0.0016. These are improvements of 27 %, 5.9 %, 6.2 % and 28 % over homogeneous texturing.
  • Clinical claim: none beyond visual resemblance.
  • Relevance: shows that DukeSim accepts learned heterogeneous phantoms. Not read: how generated HU textures were turned into DukeSim materials. That conversion is exactly the interface question for our export step.

Shankar et al. (2022) (full text via PMC excerpt)

  • Task: validate DukeSim against a physical Kyoto Kagaku chest phantom fitted with pig-lung samples and NIST foams.
  • Data: scanned on Siemens Force and Flash at 120 kV, CTDIvol 1.15–7.42 mGy, 0.75 mm slices, wFBP or iterative reconstruction. The virtual phantom was built at 0.25 mm. 28 simulations were reconstructed with vendor ReconCT.
  • Results: mean biomarker difference 3.84 % (range 0.19–18.27 %) for LAA-950, LAA-856, Perc15, lung mass, volume and HU distributions. Pig-lung inserts were excluded “due to their unknown textures”.
  • Clinical claim: none, as it uses no patients.
  • Relevance: sets the expected sim-to-real gap on lung biomarkers. It does not validate the TrueCT Duke-1 model specifically.

Ronneberger et al. (2015) (abstract)

  • Model: encoder–decoder with skip connections, trained end-to-end “from very few images” with heavy augmentation.
  • Results: won the ISBI 2015 cell-tracking challenge categories; segments a 512 × 512 image in under 1 s.
  • Relevance: the base architecture. It addresses segmentation, not quantitative regression.

Çiçek et al. (2016) (abstract)

  • Model: the 2-D U-Net with every operation replaced by its 3-D counterpart, trained from sparse annotation, with on-the-fly elastic deformation.
  • Data: Xenopus kidney.
  • Relevance: justifies the 3-D context arm of A04. No CT or regression results.

Oktay et al. (2018) (abstract)

  • Model: attention gates added to a U-Net.
  • Results: on two abdominal CT multi-class datasets, attention gates “consistently improve” U-Net across datasets and training sizes, at small cost.
  • Relevance: the attention arm of A13.

Isensee et al. (2021) (abstract; method details from preprint arXiv:1904.08128, full text)

  • What it is: a self-configuring U-Net pipeline evaluated on 23 public segmentation datasets.
  • Training defaults:
    • loss is “the sum of cross-entropy and Dice loss”, which empirically improved training stability and accuracy;
    • 1000 epochs × 250 iterations;
    • SGD with Nesterov momentum 0.99, learning rate 0.01, poly decay;
    • deep supervision.
  • CT normalisation: global (not per-image), clipping to the 0.5–99.5 foreground percentiles and then applying the global foreground mean and SD, “as intensity values are quantitative”.
  • Configurations: 2-D, 3-D full-resolution and 3-D cascade, chosen by 5-fold cross-validation. The 3-D full-resolution configuration was best overall, but the best choice per dataset “can not be predicted with perfect accuracy”.
  • Augmentation: includes Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur and low-resolution simulation.
  • Relevance: directly supports A04, A06 and A17 and the train-only global normalisation.

Ho et al. (2020) (abstract)

DDPM, trained on a weighted variational bound linked to denoising score matching. Reports FID 3.17 on CIFAR-10. Defines our diffusion objective. Unconditional natural-image generation only.

Saharia et al. (2022) (abstract)

  • Model: conditional image-to-image diffusion.
  • Results: outperforms “strong GAN and regression baselines” on colourisation, inpainting, uncropping and JPEG restoration. Reports that L1 versus L2 in the denoising objective affects sample diversity, and that self-attention matters.
  • Relevance: motivates A08 and A10. Natural images and perceptual metrics only; no quantitative-regression target.

Song et al. (2021) (abstract)

Non-Markovian sampler using the same training as DDPM. Produces samples 10–50× faster and trades compute for quality. Directly supports A09.

3. Per-paper notes: expansion papers

CT number to density and material conversion

  • Schneider et al. (1996) (abstract). Introduces the stoichiometric calibration: fit the scanner’s attenuation parameters on tissue-equivalent inserts, then compute the HU-to-electron-density (or stopping power) curve for real tissue compositions. It proved more precise than direct tissue-substitute calibration when checked by proton radiography of a biological sample.
  • Schneider et al. (2000) (abstract). Splits the HU scale into sections. Within each section, mass density and elemental weights are interpolated with two-component mixture relations. Expected accuracy: density better than 0.04 g/cm³; P and Ca within 1 and 2.3 percentage points; H within 0.8 pp and N within 3 pp; but C and O up to 14 pp. This is the classical quantitative statement that single-energy CT fixes density far better than composition, which supports the plan’s identifiability principle.

Deep learning for density and material maps from single-energy CT

  • Gao et al. (2023) (full text). SECT → mass density and RSP using a voxelwise ANN, an FCNN and a ResNet.
    • Training: only the CIRS 062M electron-density phantom (9 inserts, 0.203–1.830 g/cm³).
    • Testing: Gammex 467, plus the CIRS M701 and M702 anthropomorphic phantoms (head–neck, thorax and pelvis protocols, 120 kVp).
    • Whole-phantom results, M701 (Table 12): FCNN mass-density MAPE in lung was 1.57 %, against 8.29 % for stoichiometric calibration and 12.96 % for the voxelwise ANN. FCNN bone MAPE was 0.92 % against 3.17 %.
    • Internal inconsistency: the abstract attributes 12.96 % to the stoichiometric method in lung, but Table 12 gives 12.96 % to the ANN.
    • Patients: two patient scans were shown qualitatively, and one patient’s mean densities are tabulated. There was no patient ground truth.
    • Takeaway: convolutional context beat the voxelwise mapping, especially in lung, which is direct evidence for A02.
  • Charyyev et al. (2022) (abstract). SECT → synthetic high- and low-kV DECT with a residual attention GAN, on 70 head–neck patients with leave-one-out validation. MAE was 36.9 and 35.8 HU. SPR from a dual-energy stoichiometric method was within about 1 % NMSD of SPR from real DECT. Clinical data, but the target is a surrogate image rather than a material ground truth.

XCAT and DukeSim phantom texture and material encoding

  • Abadi, Segars, et al. (2019) (full text) and Abadi et al. (2018) / Abadi et al. (2017) (abstracts): see §1.1.
  • Abadi et al. (2023) (full text), the closest precursor to the TrueCT COPD phantoms:
    • 20 phantoms (10 XCAT bodies × 2 emphysema renditions) at 0.25 mm;
    • ICRU 46 materials; one density per phantom for emphysema and for parenchyma;
    • simulated with DukeSim modelling a Siemens Flash at 20–100 mAs;
    • wFBP B31f/B46f/B70f and SAFIRE I31/I46/I70, 0.75 mm slices at 0.5 mm interval, 0.48 mm pixels, 500 mm FOV;
    • ground truth was μ at the 67 keV effective energy, expressed in HU.
    • Best condition (100 mAs, I31): mean lung HU error 35 ± 3 HU, LAA-950 −4 ± 5 %, Perc15 26 ± 10 HU.
    • Lung MAE was 57–175 HU across conditions, larger at edges and in dense regions.

Conditional diffusion compared with deterministic and adversarial regression

  • Lyu and Wang (2022) (abstract). DDPM and score-matching models with four samplers, used for MRI → CT. They “generate better synthetic CT images than the CNN and GAN models”, and averaging Monte Carlo samples improved results. Dataset size and metrics were not read.
  • Wolleb et al. (2021) (abstract). Diffusion segmentation conditioned on the image at every sampling step. Repeated sampling gives uncertainty maps and an implicit ensemble that “increases the segmentation performance” (BRATS 2020). Published at MIDL 2022 according to secondary sources. Cited here as the arXiv version, which is what was verified.
  • Blau and Michaeli (2018) (abstract). Proves that lowering mean distortion necessarily makes outputs more distinguishable from real images, for any distortion measure. This is the theoretical reason to expect a sample mean (A10) or a deterministic U-Net (A08) to win on MAE while losing realistic texture.
  • Moschetto et al. (2025) (arXiv abstract snippet). With a shared U-Net backbone for T1w → T2w MRI, a GAN (Pix2Pix) outperformed the diffusion and flow-matching models. It is a counterexample to assuming diffusion wins on paired translation.

5. Ablation rationale (A01–A19)

“Support” means a primary source read here bears directly on the contrast. Where the plan’s listed reference does not support the ablation, this is stated.

Motivates having labels at all. No paper compares density-only, label-only and joint training. Valand et al. (2026) regresses two continuous maps, not labels; Salinas et al. A14 resolution Abadi et al. (2025) (0.25 mm phantom vs ≈ 0.98 × 0.98 × 0.55 mm CT; scoring by linear upsampling); Isensee et al. (2021) (median-spacing resampling) Good.
A15 volume-averaged vs point target Abadi et al. (2025) (the challenge itself used linear interpolation onto the phantom grid); Abadi et al. (2023) (edge-voxel errors); Abadi et al. (2018) (sub-voxel vessels and airways) Good. The listed Valand et al. (2026) does not address it.
A16 reconstruction variant Abadi et al. (2025) (5 algorithms gave widely different RMSE and SSIM); Abadi et al. (2023) (kernel and algorithm effects); Valand et al. (2026) (trained across 3 kernels) Good, conditional on variants existing locally.
A17 noise/blur augmentation Isensee et al. (2021) (Gaussian noise, blur and low-resolution augmentation are defaults); Abadi et al. (2023) Partial. Shankar et al. (2022) does not address augmentation.
A18 metadata conditioning Abadi et al. (2025) (per-case mAs varies; DICOM-CT-PD carries tube current); Valand et al. (2026) (multi-dose, multi-kernel training) Weak. No paper read conditions on metadata.
A19 seed stability Isensee et al. (2021) (5-fold ensembles as a variance reducer) Weak. Methodological norm only.

6. Where the literature questions the plan

  1. “XCAT-based voxel phantoms.” The emphysema models are new COPDGene-derived anatomies, built with XCAT-style lung and bone texture models. Only the liver-lesion models used the original XCAT library (Abadi et al. 2025).
  2. Recon provenance. The challenge distributed sinograms. The paper documents no standard “challenge-supplied reconstruction”, no kernel and no quantitative HU calibration. The recons/ folder must be characterised from its DICOM headers and from water and air ROIs, not assumed.
  3. Density varies per voxel is unlikely. Density is per material label, with texture handled as discrete label bins (bones: 5 levels), plus one emphysema density per phantom (Abadi, Segars, et al. 2019; Abadi et al. 2023). A01, A05 and A07 should be interpreted with this in mind, and E00 should confirm it.
  4. The challenge ground truth is 64 keV mono-energetic HU, not density. A physically grounded intermediate target (μ at 64 keV from label + table) is available and was the challenge’s own scoring target. The plan does not list it. It could serve as a sanity baseline at no cost.
  5. Several plan reference attributions are not supported by the text read: Valand et al. (2026) for A02, A05 and A15; Isensee et al. (2021) for A03 and A11; Shankar et al. (2022) for A17. See §5.
